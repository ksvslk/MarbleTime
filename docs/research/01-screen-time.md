# Research 1 · Screen-time guidance and evidence on earned screen time

Input for the reward/time redesign (Oct 7, 2026). Compiled by a research agent that opened every source listed (guideline text, full text or abstract) except one marked "(not opened)". Evidence tags: **[G]** official guideline or policy, **[MA]** meta-analysis or systematic review, **[RCT]**, **[L]** longitudinal observational, **[X]** cross-sectional, **[Q]** qualitative or field study, **[T]** theory.

## 1. Key findings

### A. How much screen time per day

1. **Under 5: at most 1 hour a day, and less is better. [G]**
   - WHO 2019: for 1-year-olds screen time "is not recommended"; for ages 2–4 "no more than 1 hour; less is better"; "reading and storytelling with a caregiver is encouraged".
   - The same limit elsewhere: Canadian Early Years guidelines (2017); Canadian Paediatric Society (CPS) 2022, "about 1 hour or less per day"; Sweden 2024, "max en timme skärmtid per dag"; Estonia (Tervise Arengu Instituut, TAI) 2025, ages 2–7, "kuni 60 minutit päevas, kuid mida vähem seda parem"; Norway 2025, ages 2–5, "30 min - 1 time".
   - AAP 2016: for ages 2–5, "1 hour per day of high-quality programs", watched together with a parent.
2. **School age: 1–2 hours a day of leisure screens. [G]**
   - Canada, ages 5–17 (2016): "No more than 2 hours per day of recreational screen time".
   - Sweden, ages 6–12: "max en till två timmar" (schoolwork excluded).
   - Norway, ages 6–12: "1 - 1,5 time dagleg på fritida".
   - Estonia, ages 7–18: entertainment screen time "kuni kahe tunnini päevas".
   - AAP 2026 replaces the 2016 statements and drops fixed limits; if a family wants a number: "<1 hour/day for toddlers and preschoolers to 1 to 2 hours/day or more of entertainment (not school-related) media for school-aged children".
3. **The numbers are precautions, not proven thresholds. [G/MA]**
   - AAP 2026: "the science in this field is primarily observational". WHO 2020: "evidence was insufficient to quantify a sedentary behaviour threshold". RCPCH 2019: "Evidence is weak for a threshold … We are unable to recommend a cut-off"; families should negotiate limits based on what screens push out (sleep, activity).
   - Stiglic & Viner 2019 (review of 13 reviews): "Evidence for threshold effects was weak", but more screen time is moderately strongly linked to body fat and depressive symptoms.
   - Only 35.6% of 2–5-year-olds meet ≤1 hour a day (McArthur 2022; 63 studies, n=89,163).
4. **Sleep, session length, watching together, game design. [G/MA]**
   - No screens in the last hour before bed (AAP 2026, RCPCH, CPS, Norway); Sweden says 30–60 minutes.
   - Estonia, ages 3–5 (TAI with the Estonian speech therapists' association, 2026): 1 hour a day in sessions of "maksimaalselt 10–15 minutit", with movement or play breaks; agree the time in advance and stay firm when it ends.
   - Watching together improves learning (g=0.198; Taylor 2024, 17 studies).
   - AAP 2026: low-quality design includes "frequent rewards for play"; children's games "could include less frequent reinforcement"; autoplay off.
5. **Don't use screens to calm children. [L/G]** Calming 3–5-year-olds with devices predicted more emotional outbursts later in boys (r=0.20; Radesky 2023, n=422). Sweden, Norway and the CPS advise against it.

### B. Screen time as a reward or punishment

6. **Parents who reward or punish with screen time have children with more screen time. [X; one L]**
   - Measured with two items: "I offer screen time … as a reward for good behaviour" and "I take away screen time … as a punishment".
   - Tang 2018 (n=62, ages 1.5–5): +0.15 h weekday screen time per point for mothers (95% CI 0.01–0.29). Nagata 2025 (n=10,048, ages 12–13): +0.36 h/day per point (CI 0.12–0.60), and more problem gaming. Goncalves 2023 (n=77 preschoolers): ρ=0.30.
   - Limiting and monitoring go with less screen time (Tang β −0.31 and −0.34).
   - The only longitudinal study (Neshteruk 2021, n=252, ages 2–5, 59 weeks): screen time as a reward not linked to TV hours but to higher BMI z-score (β=0.15); limiting and monitoring went with 1.79 fewer TV hours a week.
   - Limits: causality could run the other way; no study separates "earning" from "taking away".

### C. Parental mediation

7. **Meta-analysis (Collier 2016, 57 studies), small effects. [MA]** Restricting: slightly better outcomes (r=−.06). Merely co-viewing: slightly worse (r=+.09). Talking about media: lower aggression, substance use and sexual behaviour (r −.06 to −.11).
8. **How parents restrict matters more than whether they do. [X]** Van Petegem 2019 (n=762, ages 3–9): more rules → less defiance and problem gaming; a controlling style → the opposite. Beyens & Beullens 2017 (n=364, ages 2–10): heavy restriction → more conflict; using devices together → less. Nathanson 2002: restriction → more positive attitudes to the forbidden content. Livingstone 2017 (N=6,400, ages 6–14): restriction lowers risks and opportunities.
9. **Ending sessions. [Q]** Hiniker 2016 (55 families, ages 1–5): "Technology-mediated transitions are significantly more successful than parent-mediated transitions." Hiniker 2018 (24 homes): autoplay lengthened viewing and weakened self-control; a hard lock-out "did not reduce video-viewing time".

### D. Earning TV time and TV budgets

10. **Small trials: making TV depend on activity cut TV time.** Faith 2001 (n=10, obesity): TV only while pedalling; 1.6 vs 21.0 h/week. Goldfield 2006 (n=30, ages 8–12): activity earned TV via a token device; TV −116 min/day (controls +14), activity +65% (controls +16%). Jason 1985 (n=1); Wolfe 1984 (n=5): 20 *unearned* tokens a week more than halved viewing, still down a year later.
11. **The best trial used a budget, not earning. [RCT/MA]** Epstein 2008 (RCT, n=70, ages 4–7, "TV Allowance"): weekly budget cut 10% a month to −50%, $0.25 per *unused* half-hour; TV and computer time −17.5 h/week vs −5.2 for controls; BMI z-score fell over 2 years. SWITCH (n=251, ages 9–12): no significant effect. Reviews: monitoring devices and contingent feedback "most effective" (Schmidt 2012, 47 studies); overall effects small, SMD 0.116, 0.145 with goals, feedback and planning (Jones 2021, 204 studies). Every successful design kept available TV below prior viewing; none used chores; none let a child exceed the guidelines.

### E. Weekdays and weekends

12. **No guideline adds weekend time. [G/X]** AAP 2026: limits "might vary … school nights versus weekends". Preschoolers already watch more at weekends (1.88 vs 1.24 h; Tang 2018). No study supports a specific weekend extra.

## 2. Implications for MarbleTime (the agent's recommendations)

1. A hard daily maximum covering base + jobs + games + bonus, games included: ages 4–5 → 60 min (parents can lower to 30–45; base 20–30; earnable ≤30); 6–7 → 60–90 (Estonia keeps 60 until 7; base 30; earnable ≤30–45); 8–10 → 90–120 (default 90; base 30–45; earnable ≤45). A parent setting for other screens lowers the TV maximum.
2. The base is an allowance, not a wage: "today's jar fills up to a fixed top"; marbles can also buy non-screen treats; something for unused marbles (Epstein); earnable share about a third to a half of the maximum (assumption to test).
3. Remove "take earned minutes away" as discipline; if kept, only for screen-related issues with an explanation (logical consequence), never for unrelated misbehaviour or to calm a tantrum.
4. Brain games are screen time: count toward the maximum, cap their earning, avoid frequent-reward loops.
5. Let the TV end the session: countdown, stop at the end of an episode, autoplay off; 10–15-minute sessions with breaks for 4–5-year-olds.
6. Lock the TV 60 minutes before bedtime.
7. Weekends: same maximum by default; parents may set a higher weekend maximum within the age limit; no roll-over past the maximum; a one-off "special day" override.
8. Prompt watching together, without extra minutes for it.

## 3. Where evidence is weak or missing

- No study of chores earning TV time in typical 4–10-year-olds; effort-for-TV trials are tiny, mostly ages 8–12 with obesity, used physical activity, and capped TV below previous viewing; SWITCH found no effect.
- Reward/punishment studies are cross-sectional (except Neshteruk), combine reward and punishment, and rely on parent reports.
- Unknown whether earning makes TV more valued and chores less (means–end effect, Lepper 1982 — not opened).
- Maximums are expert consensus, and countries differ.
- No evidence on weekend extras, roll-over, the earned share, or whether a TV-only limit pushes children to tablets.
- Pilot measures: daily TV minutes vs before install; share of days at the maximum; fuss at session end (one-tap parent rating); chores during a week without marbles; the child's TV-vs-other choice before/after; screen use near bedtime.
