## 1. Key findings

1. **Token economies work, but nobody has studied their numbers (S/W).** Single-case meta-analysis: mean effect 0.82 (Tau); "slightly more effective for youth between the ages of 6 and 15 years than for children between the ages of 3 and 5"; adding response cost changed nothing [31]. Only 19% of 96 studies fully describe tokens, exchange schedules and backup rewards [14]. No evidence-based exchange rate exists.
2. **Consistency, immediacy and small, frequent payoffs (S/W).** Teaching "the importance of parenting consistency" predicted larger effects across 77 parenting-programme evaluations [16]. "Immediate delivery of a token helped sustain otherwise weak behavior"; free tokens up front "resulted in fewer tokens earned"; choice of rewards should help, "although there is surprisingly little empirical support" [11]. Children 6–11 discount delayed rewards more steeply than teens [29, W]. Kazdin: rewards small "so the child can earn one nearly every day, especially at first" [1, W].
3. **Fading needs a plan (M).** Across 37 studies, the best maintenance combined "thinning or fading… with programmed transfer of control via social reinforcement or self-management" [26]. Triple P: reward charts "should be temporary" [28, W].
4. **Expected rewards undermine enjoyable activities, more so for children (S).** Preschoolers expecting an award later drew 8.59% of free time vs 16.73% (no award) and 18.09% (surprise award) [18]. Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (128 studies), effects on later free-choice play [6]:

5. **The counterpoint (S).** "Rewards given for low-interest tasks enhance free-choice intrinsic motivation"; performance-linked rewards do no harm [3]. Incentives predict quantity, intrinsic motivation quality (k=183) [5].
6. **"Do A to get B" makes children value A less (M).** Preschoolers "showed significantly less subsequent interest in the activity that they undertook first (the means)" [19].
7. **Screens as reward/punishment go with more screen use (W/M, observational).** β=0.15 (95% CI 0.01–0.29) for weekday screen time in 1.5–5-year-olds (n=62; reverse causation possible) [33]; prospective, ~age 13 (N=7,947): "greater screen and smartphone time" [23].
8. **Chores go with competence, not shown causally (M).** Kindergarten chores predicted third-grade self-competence (N=9,971; rare-chore odds ratios 1.17–1.27 for bottom-fifth scores) [37]; chores "significantly predicted working memory and inhibition" (N=207, ages 5–13, one-time survey) [34]; in 9–14-year-olds only routine family chores went with concern for others, and only in older children [9]. The "Rossmann" finding was never published [27].
9. **Paying for chores: no trials (W).** A material reward reduced 20-month-olds' later helping vs praise or nothing [35]. Reward charts can teach "one should only be helpful and kind when there are material rewards" [28].
10. **Praise the deed, not the child (M, replication caveat).** Intelligence praise reduced persistence after failure (fifth graders) [22]; person praise → more "helpless" responses at 5–6 [15]; parents' effort praise at 14–38 months predicted a growth belief at 7–8 (N=53) [10]; a plain "yea" worked as well as effort praise, "gestures produced the highest self-evaluations" (ages 5–6) [21]; over-the-top praise made low-self-esteem children avoid challenges [2]; avoid "overreliance on social comparisons" [12]; controlling feedback ("you should keep up the good work") d=−0.78 vs informational; verbal rewards did not raise children's motivation (d=0.11, n.s.) [6]. Caveat: a 624-child replication (ages 9–13) found no motivation effects [20].
11. **Taking tokens away works about as well as earning them (M).** "About equally effective", no consistent preference [13]; in ADHD, response cost worked better "but at the expense of decreased self-rated motivation on the low-interest task" [4]; government guidance: "don't remove stickers for problem behaviour" [36, W].
12. **Positive reinforcement plus mild logical consequences, never harshness (S).** 154 trials: "positive reinforcement, praise in particular, and natural/logical consequences" predicted stronger effects [17]; 1,435 studies: harsh and psychological control (guilt, shame, withdrawing love) most linked to aggression and rule-breaking [24].
13. **Random rewards resemble gambling (M, correlational).** "A variable ratio reinforcement schedule… underpins many forms of gambling"; 45% of games with loot boxes met all five gambling criteria [7]; loot-box spending linked to problem gambling at 16–18 (η²=0.120) [38]; mean r=0.27 across studies [32]; apps for 3–5-year-olds commonly use lures (45%), character pressure (25%), time pressure (17%) [25].
14. **Streaks work by fear of losing them (W for children).** Broken streaks reduce engagement (adults) [30]; EU guidelines: switch off "communication 'streaks'" for minors by default and protect them from "virtual currencies or loot-boxes" [8].

## 2. The agent's implications for MarbleTime

1. **Jobs earn marbles; brain games mainly earn progress.** Show progress and skill ("3 solved!"); if games pay marbles at all: few, fixed, capped, paid in full for an easy standard every child can reach (least harmful form, F4); never per correct answer; never "play to unlock TV" (F6); let children keep playing after the cap without marbles.
2. **Size rewards from the screen-time budget down.** The daily TV limit caps spending however full the jar is; keep the marble's value fixed; a normal day's routine jobs buy one short TV session (illustration: 60-minute limit with half earnable = 6 marbles over about 3 jobs); price games below jobs; small, occasional bonuses; 4–6-year-olds spend daily, 7–10-year-olds may save; parents can add non-screen rewards priced in marbles (park trip, choosing dinner).
3. **Fast, visible, dependable.** Acknowledge "I did it!" at once; ask for same-day approval; the marble drops on approval; never show a marble that can vanish; the same rules whichever grown-up approves.
4. **"Yay!" says what the child did and who noticed** ("Yay! You fed Rex. – Dad"; a high-five animation is fine); no trait words, no over-the-top words, no comparisons, no pressure; keep bonuses unexpected.
5. **Taking time away: keep it, but narrow it.** Never remove marbles from the jar; a parent may shorten today's TV for breaking a pre-agreed rule about TV itself (logical consequence); small, never below zero; neutral reason, no sad animation, not shown to siblings; show parents their weekly given/removed ratio and flag frequent removals.
6. **Fade the reward per job.** New jobs: a marble every time plus praise naming the deed; after steady success the job becomes "routine" (fewer marbles, self-tick, praise continues); family-wide jobs end as praised, unpaid "family helping"; extra jobs stay paid; prefer recurring jobs.
7. **Don't build:** random or mystery marbles, spin wheels, double-or-nothing, daily login rewards, breakable streaks, expiring marbles, countdowns, sibling leaderboards, guilt-tripping characters.
8. **Measure:** play after the cap without marbles; job completion after fading; weekly screen time before/after install; weekly parent removals; all by age band.

## 3. Where evidence is weak or missing

No study of apps where children earn screen time or of TV minutes as tokens (screen-as-reward data observational). Nothing supports 5 min per marble, 1–6 marbles per job, 2 per game or 4 a day: treat as settings to tune. Undermining data come from short lab sessions; whether a child enjoys the AI games is unknown (measure). No trials of paying vs not paying for chores; chore benefits correlational. Effort-praise effects did not replicate in older children. Taking screen time away at home is unstudied; "shorten today's TV, never touch the jar" is an inference. Streak data are adult; loot-box data involve real money. Few studies compare 4–6 with 7–10-year-olds.
