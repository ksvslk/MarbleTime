## 1. Key findings

### A. Do apps and TV teach?

1. **Yes, modestly. Strong.** Apps for preschool to grade 3: +0.31 SD (36 studies), more for preschoolers and basic skills [S4]. RCT, 389 4–5-year-olds: maths apps beat standard practice [S5]. Sesame Street improved literacy and numeracy in 15 countries [S6].
2. **Most apps lack thinking. Weak–moderate.** Children learn from active, engaged, meaningful, social play toward a goal [S1]. 73% of 124 top apps scored low on active learning ("drills skills… online workbook"); generic sound/visual feedback and "excessive rewards" lowered scores [S2].

### B. Feedback and attempts

3. **Explain, don't just mark. Strong.** Explanation 0.49, showing the correct answer 0.32, right/wrong alone 0.05 [S9]. Reward/punishment-only feedback d=0.24; high-information feedback 0.99 [S10].
4. **Tapping through hurts. Moderate.** Clicking answers "until an answer is identified as correct" correlated with lower post-test scores (r=−0.38; middle school) [S7].
5. **Preschool apps. Moderate.** ~47% answer errors with just "No, try again"; 81% never adapt; none get easier after failure [S3]. With 240 4–5-year-olds, explanatory feedback cut errors (when played first), and slowly rising challenge beat random order [S3]. Forced answer-until-correct breeds trial and error (Blair 2013 pilot, cited in [S3]; weak).
6. **Expert review. Moderate.** Specific feedback beats "answer until correct"; low achievers gain more from seeing the answer than from "try again"; avoid hints that always end in the answer, and praise of the child's self [S8].
7. **Immediate correction matters. Moderate.** Second graders repeated wrong options on hard items unless given immediate feedback [S11]; errors followed by correction aid learning [S12]; feedback helped 7–9-year-olds with no strategy but hurt those taught one [S13]; revision after feedback helped on open-ended, not multiple-choice items [S14].

### C. Adaptive difficulty

8. **The "85% rule" is theory**, derived for gradient-descent learners on two-choice tasks; it "remains to be generalized to … multi-choice tasks" [S15].
9. **Math Garden (>400,000 children). Moderate.** Elo ratings; sessions of 15 items, up to 20 s each; children choose about 90/75/60% success; scoring "discourages fast guessing"; a "?" button skips; ~21% of answers were unexpected errors, fast guesses among them [S16, S17].
10. **Easier practice, more learning. Moderate.** 207 children (grades 3–6) at about 60/75/90% success: higher success meant more practice and bigger gains; anxiety fell equally [S18, S20]. Choosing one's own success rate gave no benefit [S19]. A geography app: "appropriate" peaked near 65% (school users ~75%); a self-adjusting target raised median answers from 21 to 28 [S20].
11. **The Elo method. Moderate.** For k options, P(right) = 1/k + (1−1/k)·logistic(θ−d); update θ += K·(correct − P), step K = a/(1+b·n) starting at a=1, b=0.05; separate K for children and items; uncertainty grows with days away [S21].

### D. Age expectations (official curricula)

12. **Maths.** Estonia's 2025 preschool curriculum: age 6–7 counts to 12, can "liita ja lahutada viie piires" (add and subtract within 5) and continues patterns [S24]. Estonian school starters: counting 96%, sums within 5 89% (n=1,198) [S26]. School targets are per stage: end of grade 3, mental sums within 100 [S25]. US: kindergarten fluent within 5; grade 1 within 20 (fluent within 10); grade 2 fluent within 20 [S23]. Most children subitize up to 4 by about 5; progress "often inconsistent" [S22]. So "14 − 6" for an Estonian 6-year-old is about 1.5–2 years early.
13. **Reading. Moderate.** Estonian 6–7-year-olds know letters and blend 1–2-syllable words [S24]; taught in kindergarten; school at 7; Finnish children catch up by end of grade 1 [S27]. At school entry: words 83%, sentences 77%, made-up words 67%; about a third may need support [S26]. Regular spelling makes reading fluent within grade 1 [S28]. Sound awareness grows from big units to single sounds, hearing before saying, blending before splitting, first sound before last [S29].
14. **Reasoning. Moderate.** Patterning at 4 predicts grade-5 maths (n=517) [S30]. Patterns: AB → ABB/AABB; copying and extending before rebuilding with new materials or naming the unit [S31]. Young children fall for look-alike wrong answers; difficulty rises with the number of relations [S32].

### E. Sessions and motivation

15. **Sessions and read-aloud.** Sustained attention improves fast between 5–6 and 8–9 [S33]; spaced daily lessons beat one block (ages 5–7) [S34] (moderate). Three answer options is optimal (older students) [S35]; reading aloud helps, more with a human reader [S36] (strong). Time-pressure evidence sparse [S37] (weak).
16. **Praise and mindset. Mixed.** Person/intelligence praise led to "helpless" reactions after setbacks, ages 4–11 [S38–S40]; a replication (n=624) [S41] and a preregistered study (n=150) [S42] found little or no effect. Mindset programmes weak [S43]; d=0.05, not significant after bias correction [S44]; d=0.14 when targeted [S45].

## 2. Implications for MarbleTime

1. **One answer counts** (4–7): a wrong tile is marked calmly (no buzzer); show and say the right answer with a one-line reason tied to the picture; move on; ask a sibling question 2–4 items later or the next day; only first answers move the rating or earn credit; no retry (with 3 tiles it is a coin flip).
2. **Guard against guessing** (4, 9): ignore taps before the read-aloud ends and very fast taps; shuffle the right tile's position; repeated fast misses → suggest a break and tell the parent; optional "?" skip without penalty.
3. **Feedback** (2, 3, 6, 7, 16): explain when the item is near or above the child's level, a brief ✓ when well below; talk about the task ("5 and 2 more is 7"), never "you're smart" or sibling comparisons; short celebrations at the end of the round.
4. **Difficulty engine** (8–11): one rating per child per skill tag with the guessing correction; target ~80–85% right on the first try (about 70–78% genuinely known with 3 tiles), easier (~90%) early on and after misses; K = a/(1+b·n), raised after days away; each question family has its own difficulty with a smaller step; related skills update together.
5. **Extensible content** (12–14): the AI declares skill tags and difficulty features; code checks them; real difficulty is learned from answers. Feature orders: numbers within 5 → 10 → 20 without crossing ten → crossing ten → 100; sounds: syllable → first sound → single sounds, blending before splitting; patterns: AB → AABB, copy → extend → rebuild → name the unit; no look-alike wrong answers for under-6s.
6. **Cold start from age** (12, 13): start one step below with high uncertainty; ~10–15 answers place the child; parents can adjust. Age 4–5: count and subitize to 5; 6: within 5; 7: within 10; 8: within 20 crossing ten; 9–10: within 100. Reading: letters and first sounds at 5–6; blending at 6–7.
7. **Sessions** (9, 15): about 5 questions at ages 4–6, 10 at ages 7–10 (3–6 minutes), daily, ending on a success; no countdown or speed scoring (judgement calls; tune with data).
8. **Read-aloud** (13, 15): auto-read question and tiles for under-8s; anyone can replay; natural or human-recorded voice; reading required only in reading games.
9. **Motivation** (2, 16): mastery per skill, not ranks; errors are normal ("Let's look"); no mindset slogans.

## 3. Where evidence is weak or missing

Attempt policies: no child RCT compares them. Target success rate for ages 4–6 unknown (Math Garden data are grades 3–6; the 85% rule is theory). Session length, item count, option count and time pressure for under-7s not studied directly. Estonia: no national grade-1 targets or sound-awareness norms; national grade-1/2 tests not opened. No Raven-style norms or difficulty data for AI-written questions. Not opened: Callaghan & Reich 2018 (dissertation read), Blair 2013, Anthony et al. 2003, Klinkenberg full text, Clements & Sarama book.
